Extending Bulk-Billing Incentives to All Patients: Evidence from Australia’s November 2025 Medicare Reform

Working paper, Monash University

On 1 November 2025 Australia extended Medicare bulk-billing incentives, previously limited to children and concession card holders, to all patients, and introduced a 12.5% loading for practices that bulk bill every eligible service.

Comparing working-age adults, who became eligible, with children, who already were, in monthly Medicare data for 393 local government areas by age group, I find that the reform raised the working-age bulk-billing rate by 6.3 percentage points (95% confidence interval 5.7 to 6.9) from a base of 69%. The effect appeared in the first month and was still growing eight months later, and it closely matches the estimate of Craig et al. (2026), obtained from the same data with a different design. The response was largest in areas where the fewest working-age bulk-billed attendances already attracted the incentive.

The paper’s main contribution is the cost accounting. Because incentive payments are observed by age group, the incentives already paid for working-age concession card holders before the reform can be netted out. I show that the cost per additional bulk-billed attendance depends on the effect, the counterfactual bulk-billing rate and the share of attendances already eligible, and that assuming no prior eligibility raises the cost by about half. The incentive cost of an additional bulk-billed attendance is about $187 ($171 to $207), against $322 in Craig et al. (2026), and 88% of the additional incentive spending went to attendances that would have been bulk billed anyway.

I also show that estimates of the November 2023 tripling from the same age-group contrast depend heavily on the pre-period: a six-month window reproduces the 3-point estimate of Craig et al. (2026), while longer windows show two years of divergence beforehand. A sensitivity analysis following Rambachan and Roth (2023) shows that the jump in November 2023 is robust, but the average effect over the following two years is not.
